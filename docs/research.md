# Memoria research brief

Prepared 14 November 2026 for the HackPrinceton Fall 2026 Education track.

## 1. Bottom line

The method of loci is a 2,000-year-old technique with real but modest-quality modern evidence: two meta-analyses report medium-to-large gains in recall of ordered lists, and both warn of a high risk of bias in the underlying studies. Memoria is defensible if it presents the palace as an encoding aid for ordered, discrete facts and makes retrieval practice and spaced review, which have the stronger evidence, the core loop.

**How this was verified.** Page fetching was blocked by this session's network allowlist, so every source was checked through web-search extracts (abstracts, PubMed and publisher records, quoted passages), not by opening full texts. "High" confidence means the claim appeared in an abstract or a quoted primary passage. Numbers that came only from press coverage are labelled "press". Anything not seen is marked UNVERIFIED. Re-check the items marked † against the full text before printing them on a slide.

**Competitive gap (row 28).** AI-assisted palace tools already exist, so that is not our difference. In this search we did not find a free tool that combines a ready-made walkable route in the browser (no headset, no install), learner-edited images, a hidden-image recall walk and scheduled spaced reviews in one loop. That is a search result, not proof of absence, and should be worded that way.

## 3. Design principles

1. Every palace is a fixed route with one fact per locus, and the order of loci never changes. (Rows 1, 2, 16, 27)
2. Every locus must be visually distinct in shape, colour and light, moderately lit, evenly spaced and free of crowds or clutter, and every fifth locus carries a landmark. (Rows 2, 3, 14)
3. First-time users take a short familiarization walk of the empty route, and pass a quick route check, before any image is placed. (Rows 10, 12, 14)
4. The Architect proposes; the learner always approves, edits or replaces each image and confirms where it sits. (Rows 5, 12, 22, 23)
5. Every image must be concrete, doing something, different from its neighbours, and tied to the meaning of the fact by a one-line reason; weirdness alone is not enough. (Rows 4, 24, 25, 26)
6. Every encoding walk is followed by a recall walk with images hidden, and the learner must answer before anything is revealed. (Rows 17, 20)
7. Every review is a retrieval attempt scheduled at expanding intervals, and success is judged on delayed recall. (Rows 8, 17, 21)
8. One route per topic: never load a second, confusable list onto a route that is still being learned. (Rows 2, 18)

## 4. Pitch lines

1. "The oldest surviving description of the method of loci is in the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, written around the 80s BCE, and our palace follows the rules for places found there and in Cicero: a fixed order, distinct forms, no crowds, and a marker on every fifth place."
2. "Two meta-analyses report that the method of loci improves recall of lists by a medium-to-large margin (g = 0.65 across 13 randomised trials; d = 0.88 for immediate serial recall against rehearsal), but both flag a high risk of bias and the 2025 review grades the evidence as low to very low quality."
3. "A 2012 study of 142 participants found that a virtual environment explored for about five minutes served as well as a familiar real place, which is why we use a fixed virtual route with a familiarization walk; that study used word lists that participants imagined, so our rendered images go beyond what it tested."
4. "The palace only helps you encode; what the literature shows makes memories last is retrieval practice and spacing, so every palace ends in a recall walk with the images hidden and scheduled reviews. We have not yet run an efficacy study of Memoria itself."

## 5. Honest caveats

- Memoria is a hackathon prototype with no outcome data of its own. Every efficacy claim is borrowed from studies of the technique, not of this app.
- The evidence base is mostly short laboratory studies with university students memorizing word lists. Both meta-analyses report a high risk of bias, and classroom evidence is thin (one small medical-school study on one topic).
- Loci suit ordered, discrete facts. They do not teach understanding, and the best-known review of study techniques rates the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text as low utility while rating practice testing and spacing as high utility.
- A browser is the desktop condition. The one head-to-head study we found reported head-mounted VR better than desktop, and other work suggests navigation itself may not be the active ingredient, so the 3D walk is there for structure and engagement, not as a proven memory boost.
- AI-supplied images are not known to work as well as images the learner invents. The research on supplied versus self-made mnemonics is mixed, which is why the learner sees every image before it is placed and can rewrite any of them.
- The large gains in training studies followed about six weeks of daily practice in young adult men. Benefits are smaller after brief instruction, vary with age, and have not reliably transferred to untrained material.

## 6. Leads that did not check out

- **"Ad Herennium III, sections 16-24."** Those are chapter numbers (xvi-xxiv). The standard citation for the memory section is III.xvi.28-xxiv.40; the image rules are III.xxii.35-37.
- **"Moderately lit, moderate size."** Consistent with every summary found, but the Caplan sentences were not seen; only the rules on distinct form, deserted places, thirty feet and the fifth-locus markers were seen in Caplan's wording. UNVERIFIED at sentence level.
- **"Emotional or comic" images.** The text extracts speak of striking, active, beautiful or ugly, ornamented or disfigured images. "Comic" was seen only in paraphrase and "emotional" looks like a modern gloss (Yates). Do not put either in quotation marks.
- **Dresler 2017 effect size.** The design, sample and four-month link are confirmed. The size of the gain (roughly doubling on a 72-word test) comes from press coverage, and the group sizes and the working-memory control's task type were not confirmed.
- **Wagner 2021.** Real (Science Advances 7(10), eabc7606). Per-group word counts come from press, and it appears to share the Dresler training design, which we could not confirm.
- **"Ondřej et al. 2025."** Real, but single-authored (Jan Ondřej, *British Journal of Psychology*).
- **Krokos 2018 "8.8%."** Comes from the University of Maryland press release; we could not confirm whether it is relative or percentage points.
- **Legge 2012 as proof that rendered virtual palaces work.** It shows a virtual environment can supply the loci; items were imagined, not displayed.
- **"Self-generated images beat provided ones."** Not supported as stated. The generation effect is solid for simple verbal materials, but direct comparisons of self-made and supplied mnemonics are mixed.
- **"Bizarre images are remembered better."** Only under conditions: mixed lists and free recall. A palace in which every image is bizarre is close to the condition where the effect disappears.
- **Dunlosky 2013 as a verdict on loci.** It rated the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text, not the method of loci.
- **Macunx VR, Munx VR, Linguisticator VR as three products.** One lineage: Macunx was the 2016 Kickstarter name, Munx VR the Steam release, Linguisticator the publisher. No recent updates were found.
- **Anki "uses FSRS by default."** Confirmed only as optional since version 23.10; sources conflict on default status.
- **Quizlet and RemNote features, the Leitner book title, Massen 2006, Roediger 1980 and Paivio's primary texts.** Not seen directly in this pass; cited from secondary sources or standard references. memoryOS's "70% improvement" figure is marketing copy with no study behind it that we could find.

## 8. Part 2: diagnostic, repair, rooms and links

Added on the evening of 14 November 2026 for the "Part 2" direction: a diagnostic survey walk before any study, a palace whose state shows what is known (ruin, under repair, restored, with score history), categories as separate rooms with relationships drawn between places, a choice of architectural style, and extension up to 30 places. Sections 1 to 7 are unchanged. Rows 29 to 51 continue the numbering of section 2; where a row adds detail to an earlier row it says so.

### 8.1 Bottom line

The diagnostic survey is the best-supported part of Part 2. Trying to answer before studying is a studied effect (pretesting, or prequestioning): it improves later memory for the facts that were asked about, even when the attempt fails, provided the answers are studied afterwards, and it does nothing for facts that were not asked about. Our arrangement (nothing revealed during the survey, study walk straight after) matches the standard prequestion design used with texts and lectures; it is less secure for bare vocabulary pairs, where the classic studies needed the answer at once. Deciding what to study from answers, not from the learner's own sense of knowing, is well supported. The other ideas are not established: evidence that progress displays improve learning is thin, and no study we found tests category rooms, links drawn between places, or adding places to an existing route in a memory palace. Those are design hypotheses with indirect support and must be described that way.

**How Memoria's Part 2 differs.** Mnemorium is a desktop tool for people who want to build palaces out of photographs of their own rooms, with AI help and FSRS reviews. memoryOS is a mobile course that teaches memory techniques, largely through lessons and palaces it supplies. Memoria Part 2 runs in a browser with no install, works on the learner's own notes, and starts by finding out what the learner does not know: a survey walk with an "I don't know" option, after which the state of each place (ruined, under repair, restored) follows the learner's answers, categories sit in separate rooms and relationships are drawn between places. ### 8.5 Sentences we must not say

- "The survey improves your memory", "pretesting is proven", or any pretesting claim on a screen where study does not follow. The benefit depends on studying the answers afterwards (row 33).
- "Getting it wrong helps you learn" or "errors make memories stronger" without the condition. The finding is that a failed attempt followed by the answer beats no attempt (rows 29, 30).
- "The survey helps you learn the whole topic." Only the facts asked about benefit (row 32).
- "g = 0.54" for vocabulary, for memory palaces, or without "for the material asked about, in text and video lessons".
- "Guessing is better than saying I don't know", or the reverse. The one study found shows no difference from forcing a guess (row 35).
- "Works even for words you have never seen." Recognition only, in the studies found (row 34).
- "Memoria knows what you know." It knows what you answered; a correct multiple-choice answer can be a lucky guess.
- "Watching your palace rebuild keeps you motivated", "improves retention", or "proven gamification". Progress-display evidence is thin and the repair mechanic is untested (rows 39 to 41).
- "Gamification improves learning by half a standard deviation" without the heterogeneity and rigour caveats (row 39).
- "Sorting facts into rooms is proven to improve recall", "spatial organisation is shown to work in memory palaces", or "links between places help you understand". Hypotheses (rows 42 to 46).

### 8.6 Recommended changes to the principles and the product

Proposed wording for the Lead; CLAUDE.md is not ours to edit.

**Principles**

- **Amend 1.** Add: "A palace grows only by adding places at the end of the route or in a new room; a place in use never moves and never takes a second fact." (Rows 1, 2, 48, 49)
- **Amend 3.** Add: "The survey walk may serve as the familiarization walk, since the route is empty either way, but the route check stays, and any section added later gets its own walk before images are placed." The first clause is a judgment call with no direct evidence; the rest follows rows 10 and 14.
- **Amend 8.** Add: "Each category gets its own room, and rooms holding similar categories must differ clearly in form, colour and light." (Rows 42, 43, 44)
- **New 9.** "Every new deck starts with a survey walk: one question at each place, answered or marked 'I don't know' before moving on, with nothing revealed; the study walk follows in the same session and gives each answer at the place where it was asked." (Rows 29 to 35)
- **New 10.** "The state of a place comes only from the learner's answers, never from self-rating; a place is restored by a correct recall after study, and a correct survey answer still gets a scheduled check." (Rows 37, 38)

**Product, in order of importance for tonight**

1. **Never let the survey stand alone.** The study walk must follow in the same session, and the survey's end screen should lead straight into it. A survey with no answers afterwards has no demonstrated benefit (rows 32, 33). If a learner quits after the survey, show the answers to the missed facts before leaving, or mark the deck as "survey only, not yet studied".
2. **Should the survey show the right answer?** Not during the walk: asking first and teaching afterwards is the standard design and keeps the diagnosis clean. But the evidence leans towards sooner being better (rows 31, 32), so the answer should arrive within minutes, at the same place. In the study walk, show the survey question and the learner's own earlier answer beside the correct one; one study found that bringing back the original context with the answer is what made delayed answers work for word pairs (row 31, Zawadzka et al. 2023). For decks that are bare vocabulary pairs the support is weaker; that is a caveat to state, not a reason to change the design tonight.
3. **"I don't know" should be visible and never the default.** Nothing preselected; the copy invites an attempt first (sentence 2). Do not force a guess: forcing one made no difference in the one study found (row 35), and it would corrupt the diagnosis.
4. **Treat a correct survey answer as unconfirmed.** With multiple choice, one answer in four is right by chance. A place answered correctly in the survey can look intact but should enter the review schedule in the first Leitner box and be checked at the next review before it counts as known (rows 37, 38). Typed answers diagnose better than multiple choice where the deck allows.
5. **Keep ruin informational.** Three plain states with a legend; no points, badges, streaks or leaderboards (row 41). A place should never fall back into ruin as a punishment for one missed review without the legend explaining it as "due".
6. **Rooms.** Label each room with its category, keep three to five places per room (row 27), and make rooms for similar categories look different (row 43). Spanish tenses for the same verbs are exactly the confusable case.
7. **Links.** Present them as a view of the notes' structure, not as a memory aid. If time allows, ask at least one relation in the recall walk ("what does this step lead to?"), because retrieval beat concept mapping in the one direct comparison (row 45). Links that cross rooms are the untested extreme (row 44).
8. **Extension.** Append only; keep the landmark on every fifth place through to 30 (row 3); run the familiarization walk for the new section; say "up to 30 places" as a product limit, not as a finding.
9. **Score history.** Plot recall-walk scores by date, and label the survey score as "before study" so the before and after comparison is honest (row 36). No claim that the chart improves learning.

### 8.7 Honest caveats for Part 2

- Nothing in Part 2 has been tested in Memoria, and three of its ideas (repair display, category rooms, drawn links) have not been tested in any memory-palace study we could find.
- The pretesting literature is mostly laboratory work with texts, lectures, trivia questions and word pairs, and its effect sizes come from those materials. Our survey happens inside a 3D walk, on facts extracted from the learner's notes, sometimes as bare vocabulary. That is an extrapolation.
- The method-of-loci evidence in sections 2 and 5 is about ordered lists. Using rooms for categories and lines for relations moves away from the material for which loci were shown to help.
- The gamification and dashboard literatures rest on weak designs, short durations and novelty effects, and one classroom study found leaderboards and badges did harm.
- Competitor descriptions come from vendor pages, store listings and a competitor's comparison article, read through search extracts on one day. Features and prices change.
- No full text was opened in this pass. Every number in 8.2 comes from an abstract or a secondary extract and should be re-checked before it is printed.

### 9.5 Principles and sentences

**Principles for built-in decks** (proposed wording for the Lead; CLAUDE.md is not ours to edit)

- **9a.** "A gloss that is used as a question fits exactly one item in its deck: where a language has several words for 'you', the gloss names the one meant." (Rows S2 to S15)
- **9b.** "A reason states a word origin only when a dictionary gives it; every other link to a name is labelled 'by sound'." (Rows K20 to K27. The decks already do this.)
- **9c.** "A link that describes regulation names the enzyme acted on, not the product, and says 'inhibits', not 'blocks'." (Rows K10, K11)

**Sentences we may say**

1. "Every statement in the two showcase decks was checked against standard references: Berg and colleagues' *Biochemistry* (8th edition) and the IUBMB enzyme list for the cycle, the Real Academia Española's model conjugation for the verb, and Merriam-Webster and Etymonline for word origins. We checked search extracts of those sources, not the full texts."
2. "Where a memory image uses a real word origin (citrus, amber, smoke, apple, vinegar), a dictionary gives that origin; where it only sounds like the name (the ox in oxaloacetate, the koala in CoA), the deck says 'by sound'."
3. If asked about the feedback link: "Succinyl-CoA inhibits citrate synthase, the enzyme that makes citrate. It also inhibits alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, the enzyme that makes succinyl-CoA itself, which Berg and Tymoczko give as one of the cycle's two main control points."
4. If asked about vosotros: "The deck leaves out vosotros, the informal plural 'you' of most of Spain. In American Spanish ustedes is the only plural 'you', and it takes the same verb form as ellos."

**Sentences we must not say**

- "Succinyl-CoA blocks citrate", "destroys citrate" or "switches the cycle off". It inhibits an enzyme.
- Any mechanism for that inhibition as our own statement. One 1972 study of rat heart mitochondria reports it as competitive with acetyl-CoA; we read only an abstract record, and one web page describes it differently.
- "ATP inhibits citrate synthase." The deck does not say it; sources conflict (9.7).
- "Glue is where the word glutarate comes from." Dictionaries disagree; the deck says "by sound" and should keep saying it.
- "Checked against the textbook" without "search extracts".
- "The Spanish past tense" for the first room in front of a Spanish teacher. It is the preterite, one of several past tenses.

### 9.7 Leads that did not check out

- **"Succinyl-CoA blocks citrate."** No source says this. They say it inhibits citrate synthase.
- **How succinyl-CoA inhibits citrate synthase.** LaNoue 1972 reports inhibition competitive with acetyl-CoA; one web page says it works by blocking the release of CoA. Do not state a mechanism.
- **"ATP inhibits citrate synthase."** Listed in several study guides and in the Lehninger passage; LaNoue 1972 concluded that the energy-linked inhibition was not a direct effect of ATP on the enzyme. Not in the deck; do not add it.
- **Voet's phrase "competitive feedback inhibition".** Not found.
- **Glutarate from "glue".** Merriam-Webster says "probably" from gluten; Webster 1913 says glutamic + tartaric. That the Latin word gluten means glue was not checked in this pass. Leave the image as "by sound".
- **Fumitory as "smoke of the earth" (fumus terrae).** Not seen. Merriam-Webster gives only *Fumaria*, from the Late Latin for fumitory, from *fumus*. The deck does not use the phrase.
- **OpenStax on step six.** The adapted text calls the oxidation of succinate "a dehydration process". It is a dehydrogenation (two hydrogens to FAD), as the same passage goes on to say. Do not copy that word.
- **Which page of Berg's eighth edition holds the summary sentence** (succinate oxidised, fumarate hydrated, malate oxidised). The extract did not say; it is cited as "B8 summary".
